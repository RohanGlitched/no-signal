Game 1 · two players

Telepathy

Alice and Bob each get a card, 0 or 1. They answer 0 or 1. They win if their answers match, unless both cards are 1: then they must differ.

Your turn

Play twenty rounds as Alice. You see only your own card; Bob follows the plan you agreed before the game. A quantum team plays the same deals, with answers taken from real recorded rounds. Try to keep up.

Try it without talking

Decide in advance what each player answers for each card. Every strategy loses at least one of the four card pairs, so the best you can do is 75%. Shared coin flips don't help: they only choose between these strategies.

Now share a Bell pair

Before the game, Alice and Bob each take one qubit of an entangled pair. When the card arrives, each measures their qubit along an angle chosen for that card, and answers with the result. Their answers match with probability cos²(angle difference). Turn the dials: the best you can reach is cos²(22.5°) = 85.4%, Tsirelson's bound.

Game 2 · three players

Three of a kind

Alice, Bob and Carol each get a card. Either all three are 0, or exactly two are 1. They win if the number of 1s they answer is even when all cards are 0, and odd otherwise.

Try it without talking

Pick each player's answer for each card. Add up the four rules and every player's answers cancel out, while the rules ask for an odd total: one card set always fails. Best: 75%.

Now share a GHZ state

Three qubits entangled together, one per player. A qubit can be read in different ways; X and Y are two of them, like asking two different yes-or-no questions. Each player reads their qubit as X or Y depending on their card. With X on card 0 and Y on card 1, the product of the three results is fixed by quantum mechanics, so the team wins every round. Try other choices to see them fail.

Game 3 · two players

Magic square

Alice gets a row of a 3×3 grid and Bob gets a column. Alice fills her row with 0s and 1s, with an even number of 1s. Bob fills his column with an odd number. They win if they agree on the square where row and column cross.

Try to fill the grid

A classical team is just a grid they agreed on. Click squares to flip them. All three rows even means an even total; all three columns odd means an odd total. Both can't hold, so one of the nine row and column pairs always loses. Best: 8 in 9.

Now share two Bell pairs

Alice and Bob each hold two qubits, from two Bell pairs. Each square of the grid becomes a question asked of two qubits at once, written below as a pair of X, Y and Z readings (the Mermin–Peres square). The questions in a row can all be asked together and their answers always multiply to +1, an even row; in a column they multiply to −1, an odd column. Each player asks a whole row or column, and where they cross, entanglement makes them agree every time.

Why this is not cheating

Bob can't tell what Alice got

Entanglement correlates answers, but it carries no message. From the same Telepathy rounds, Bob answers 1 about half the time, whichever card Alice holds. If he could tell, they could talk faster than light. In a simulator this is a sanity check rather than a discovery: quantum mechanics guarantees it, so a gap bigger than chance would point to a bias in the machine or the scoring. On IonQ, each of Alice’s cards is played on a different pair of qubits, so the two bars come from different qubits.

Noise

How much noise can entanglement take?

Noise washes the shared state toward random bits. Drag the visibility down and watch each game fall to its classical limit at a different point: Three of a kind holds on down to 50% visibility, Telepathy needs 71%, and the magic square, which spends two Bell pairs on every cell it shares, needs 85%. The diamonds are the recorded machines, each placed at the visibility its Telepathy score implies. On the other two curves that is a prediction: a diamond above the line beat the simple model, one below it lost to it.

The model, for physicists

Model: each Bell pair becomes v·|Φ⁺⟩⟨Φ⁺| + (1 − v)·I/4, and the GHZ state likewise with I/8. Telepathy then wins ½ + (cos²(π/8) − ½)·v and Three of a kind ½ + v/2. In the square, four cells depend on one pair and five on both, so it wins (4(1 + v) + 5(1 + v²))/18. The tests check these curves against Qiskit Aer.

How it works

  1. One job, every game

    IonQ's runners take one job of one circuit, so the lab packs the games side by side: 4 Bell pairs for Telepathy, 4 GHZ states for Three of a kind and two magic-square rounds, 28 qubits on a Forte. Local simulators run every card set as its own small circuit.

  2. Cards are fixed per group

    Each group of qubits is wired to one set of cards, and every shot plays all of them, so each card set gets the same number of rounds. The win rate is the average over card sets, as if the referee picked them at random.

  3. Scoring

    For Telepathy, the Bell parameter S = E₀₀ + E₀₁ + E₁₀ − E₁₁ equals 8 × win − 4: classical teams stay at or below 2, quantum mechanics allows up to 2√2 ≈ 2.83. The margin over the classical bound is reported in standard errors.

  4. Classical baselines

    The classical bounds are checked by brute force over every deterministic strategy: 16 for Telepathy, 64 for Three of a kind, 4,096 grid pairs for the magic square. The race replays the same cards against a best classical strategy.

What this shows, and what it doesn't

  • No loophole-free claim. A real Bell test separates the players far enough that no signal could pass, and catches every particle, as Hensen et al. did in 2015. The 2022 Nobel Prize went to Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger for this line of experiments. Here every qubit sits in one machine, or one simulation, so the games show quantum correlations, not a proof that nature is nonlocal.
  • Simulators are models. On a noise-model simulator the numbers come from IonQ's or IBM's model of their hardware, not from ions or circuits.
  • Packed circuits. On IonQ all groups share one circuit, so crosstalk between them on real hardware would count as noise.
